# Executive Perspective Knowledge Base

This reference distills the supplied executive perspective documents into decision procedures for all nine supported audiences. Use it only after extracting the user's facts. It changes what to foreground and question, never what is true.

## Shared operating rules

1. Separate **activity**, **outcome**, **impact**, and **evidence**. Never present one as another.
2. Give an overall judgment only when the evidence supports it. Otherwise state the evidence gap and the validation needed.
3. Use role-specific metrics only when the user supplied them. Never invent a baseline, target, cost, return, deadline, or risk rating.
4. Keep the report's fixed eight-section structure. The role models below control emphasis inside those sections; they do not add or remove sections.
5. Select one primary lens. Use another lens only to identify a tradeoff, not to produce a blended report.

## CEO: strategic value and resource allocation

### Core judgment

Decide whether the work creates enough business and strategic value to justify continued investment. Convert information into a business judgment:

`What happened -> What changed -> Why it matters -> Is it worth continued investment?`

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Business outcome: revenue, growth, retention, efficiency, customer value, risk reduction, or strategic position.
2. Measurable evidence that the outcome occurred.
3. Connection to a company priority.
4. Resources consumed and opportunity cost.
5. Whether the next investment is reversible and what it unlocks.

### Diagnostic

- **Strong:** clear business outcome, supporting evidence, strategic fit, and a credible next investment decision.
- **Needs validation:** outcome exists but impact, scale, or continuation logic is unproven.
- **At risk:** execution is visible but business value or strategic fit is not.

### Frame the report

- Lead with the bottom-line business judgment, not completion status.
- Put the strongest business outcome and its evidence first.
- Label unquantified impact explicitly and name the metric needed.
- Treat opportunity cost as a decision input, not an optional footnote.

### Questions and asks

- What changed because of this work?
- Which company priority does it advance?
- What are we choosing not to fund or pursue?
- Should we continue, validate, optimize, or stop?
- Shape the Ask around strategic priority, investment level, or cross-company alignment.

### Red flags

- Completed work with no business outcome.
- Claimed impact without a measurement path.
- No accounting for resources or alternatives.
- A recommendation that does not follow from the evidence.

## CFO: financial return and capital efficiency

### Core judgment

Decide what the work is economically worth and whether it is the best use of people, time, and budget. CEO asks whether the work is worth doing; CFO asks what it costs, what it returns, and when it pays back.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Investment: people cost, time, engineering capacity, marketing spend, vendor cost, and opportunity cost.
2. Return: revenue, cost savings, productivity, retention, conversion, or explicitly identified strategic value.
3. Cost efficiency and lower-cost alternatives.
4. Payback timing and assumptions.
5. Financial risks such as overrun, low adoption, lock-in, churn, or sunk cost.

### Diagnostic

- **Invest:** return is supported, efficiency is acceptable, and no better use of the same resources is evident.
- **Validate:** potential value exists but the business case is not measured.
- **Optimize:** direction is sound but cost or operating leverage must improve.
- **Stop / Reallocate:** expected return is insufficient or a materially better alternative exists.

Use the source formula only when both inputs are supplied:

`ROI = (Return - Cost) / Cost`

If inputs are absent, write `ROI: Unquantified - requires [specific cost and return data]`. Never estimate them silently.

### Frame the report

- Lead with cost-versus-return status and the confidence level.
- Separate direct financial return from indirect or strategic value.
- State which assumptions could turn the investment into sunk cost.
- Name the cheapest credible validation before requesting scale.

### Questions and asks

- What did this cost, and what value came back?
- Is there a cheaper or faster way to achieve the same result?
- When will it pay back?
- What is the opportunity cost of continuing or stopping?
- Shape the Ask around budget, spend reduction, validation funding, or resource reallocation.

### Red flags

- ROI claimed without cost and return data.
- High burn on an outcome that has no validation plan.
- `Impact unknown` without a measurement owner or next step.
- Past spend used as the reason to continue.

## CMO: market demand, positioning, and growth

### Core judgment

Decide whether the target customer understands and wants the value, whether the market position is stronger, and whether the work can move a growth funnel. Product readiness is not market traction.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Target customer and the value they perceive.
2. External signals: adoption, interviews, sales feedback, sentiment, or behavior.
3. Funnel stage affected: awareness, acquisition, activation, engagement, retention, or conversion.
4. Positioning and differentiation against alternatives.
5. Go-to-market alignment across audience, value proposition, message, channel, and timing.
6. Opportunities to amplify the result through content, cases, community, or distribution.

### Diagnostic

- **Strong:** customer need, external signal, differentiation, and go-to-market path are coherent.
- **Needs validation:** product value may exist, but audience, message, channel, or adoption evidence is weak.
- **At risk:** work was built in isolation, has no target customer, or assumes launch equals demand.

### Frame the report

- Lead with customer or market change, not internal readiness.
- Distinguish internal enthusiasm from external validation.
- Identify exactly which funnel stage should move.
- State any go-to-market gap as audience, message, channel, timing, or measurement.

### Questions and asks

- Who is the target customer, and why do they care?
- Why would they choose this over an alternative?
- Which funnel stage moved or should move?
- How will the market discover and understand the value?
- Shape the Ask around research, messaging, launch timing, channel, or marketing resources.

### Red flags

- Launch completion reported as growth.
- Positive product feedback treated as adoption.
- No target audience or differentiated value proposition.
- Product and marketing messages conflict.

## COO: execution health and operating leverage

### Core judgment

Decide whether the organization is executing the plan efficiently and whether ownership, dependencies, and decisions are strong enough to deliver. CEO asks whether to do it; COO asks whether it is being done well.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Commitment versus actual progress and remaining schedule buffer.
2. Active blockers versus future risks.
3. Dependency chain: who is waiting for whom and why.
4. A single DRI for each decision and next action.
5. Execution efficiency: rework, meeting load, handoffs, and decision latency.
6. Resource placement and recurring process failures.

### Diagnostic

- **On track:** schedule, ownership, dependencies, and mitigation are clear; no hidden surprises.
- **Needs attention:** pressure or coordination gaps exist but have an owner and credible mitigation.
- **At risk:** timeline is slipping, ownership is unclear, or unresolved cross-team blockers require escalation.

### Frame the report

- Lead with `On track`, `Needs attention`, or `At risk`, then name the primary driver.
- Label a current stop as **Blocker** and a possible future problem as **Risk**.
- Make dependency statements directional: `[Team A] needs [input] from [Team B] by [time supplied by user]`.
- Pair every operational issue with an owner or mark ownership as missing.

### Questions and asks

- Will the commitment land, and how much buffer remains?
- Who is waiting for whom?
- Who owns the next decision?
- What single escalation would create the most movement?
- Shape the Ask around coordination, scope, decision escalation, ownership, or resources.

### Red flags

- Many involved teams but no DRI.
- Repeated blockers without attempted mitigation.
- Timeline risk raised only after the deadline is affected.
- Recurring process waste with no corrective action.

## CPO: user value and product judgment

### Core judgment

Decide whether the team is solving a real user problem, producing behavior change, and reducing product uncertainty fast enough to justify continued investment.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Problem evidence: target user, frequency, severity, current workaround, and why it fails.
2. Delivered user value: less time or effort, higher success or confidence, or new capability.
3. Behavior change: adoption, engagement, retention, conversion, or task success.
4. Product strategy and differentiation.
5. Prioritization tradeoff using impact, confidence, and effort as inputs, not fabricated scores.
6. Experience quality: clarity, discoverability, usability, accessibility, and consistency.
7. Learning velocity: which uncertainty was reduced.

### Diagnostic

- **Double down:** problem, value, behavior change, and strategic fit have supporting evidence.
- **Validate:** direction is promising but confidence is too low to scale.
- **Iterate:** the problem is credible but the experience or value delivery is weak.
- **Deprioritize:** the problem is unvalidated, adoption is persistently weak, or the work does not serve the core user or strategy.

### Frame the report

- Lead with the user problem and current validation status.
- Separate stated preference from observed behavior.
- Name what the team learned, not only what it shipped.
- Explain why this problem is prioritized now and what was deferred.

### Questions and asks

- What proves the problem is real and important?
- Did user behavior change?
- What uncertainty did this work reduce?
- What would justify scaling, iterating, or stopping?
- Shape the Ask around research, experiment direction, product tradeoff, or roadmap priority.

### Red flags

- Feature count used as product progress.
- Positive comments treated as proof of need.
- Adoption promised but not measured.
- Roadmap growth without explicit deprioritization.

## CTO: sustainable technical capability

### Core judgment

Decide whether the solution is feasible, reliable, secure, maintainable, and scalable at an acceptable engineering and operating cost. Distinguish `works for MVP` from `proven for production`.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Feasibility at the intended scale: performance, latency, throughput, and cost.
2. Reliability and observability: errors, monitoring, alerting, and incident readiness.
3. Scalability and known bottlenecks.
4. Technical debt, maintenance burden, and a credible retirement plan.
5. Architecture, reuse, and platform leverage.
6. Security, privacy, compliance, and access control.
7. Engineering efficiency and automation.
8. Build-versus-buy assumptions, dependencies, and operational ownership.

### Diagnostic

- **Scale:** target-scale behavior, reliability, observability, and ownership are supported.
- **Validate:** MVP works but scale, reliability, security, or cost assumptions remain untested.
- **Refactor:** value is real but debt or operating burden must be reduced before expansion.
- **Reconsider:** the path has critical feasibility, security, scale, or cost risk.

### Frame the report

- Lead with the most consequential technical capability or risk.
- State tested limits separately from assumptions.
- Make debt concrete: what is temporary, what burden it creates, and when it must be addressed if supplied.
- Include operating ownership, not only implementation ownership.

### Questions and asks

- What fails first as usage grows, and can we detect it?
- Is the architecture reusable or a one-off?
- Should we build, buy, or defer?
- What debt or security risk are we accepting?
- Shape the Ask around architecture, capacity, vendor choice, risk acceptance, or debt sequencing.

### Red flags

- Feature complete without production observability.
- Scale assumptions presented as test results.
- Debt recorded indefinitely without a trigger for repayment.
- Security or privacy deferred until after launch.

## Direct Manager: commitments, ownership, and development

### Core judgment

Decide whether the individual is moving important commitments forward with clear ownership, sound judgment, effective collaboration, and appropriate escalation.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Progress against commitment: on track, needs attention, or at risk.
2. Outcome and impact, including qualitative impact when metrics do not exist.
3. Ownership and initiative: what the individual noticed, decided, or drove.
4. Collaboration and ability to unblock others.
5. Risks identified and mitigation already attempted.
6. The specific support needed from the manager.

### Diagnostic

- **Strong Progress:** materially advanced important work and demonstrated ownership.
- **On Track:** commitments are progressing without a material gap.
- **Needs Attention:** progress exists, but outcome, ownership, or mitigation has a clear gap.
- **At Risk:** progress, quality, or expected result is materially threatened.

### Frame the report

- Lead with status against the commitment.
- Use active ownership language only when the facts show it.
- Preserve the boundary between what the individual owns and what depends on others.
- Include ordinary execution detail only when it explains a result, risk, judgment, or support need.

### Questions and asks

- Are you on track against the commitment?
- What is blocked, and what have you already tried?
- What do you own, and what depends on others?
- What did you learn or decide?
- Shape the Ask as `I need [decision/support] by [time, if supplied] so that [outcome]`.

### Red flags

- Meeting, document, or iteration counts without an outcome.
- Blockers listed without attempted mitigation.
- No owner for the next action.
- `Let me know if you have questions` used instead of a concrete Ask.

## VP Design: user insight, experience quality, and design leverage

### Core judgment

Decide whether design work turned user evidence into a clearer, more coherent experience and helped the organization make or implement a better decision. Design output is not design impact.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. User insight: who was observed, what friction appeared, and how strong the evidence is.
2. Experience outcome: improved clarity, usability, accessibility, trust, task success, or reduced effort.
3. Decision quality: which ambiguity, tradeoff, or product question design resolved.
4. Delivery movement: whether Product and Engineering aligned, implemented, or changed direction.
5. Quality and system leverage: consistency, reusable patterns, and avoided design or engineering rework.
6. Measurement or validation plan for unproven experience claims.

### Diagnostic

- **Strong:** user evidence shaped a clear decision, the experience outcome is supported, and delivery moved forward.
- **Validate:** the rationale is credible, but the user or experience outcome is not yet demonstrated.
- **Align:** the design direction is sound, but Product or Engineering alignment is blocking impact.
- **Rework:** output exists, but rationale, quality, consistency, or implementation readiness has a material gap.

### Frame the report

- Lead with the user or product decision design changed, not artifact volume.
- Separate design rationale from observed user evidence.
- State the quality bar or principle being protected when it affects a tradeoff.
- Describe cross-functional movement as an outcome only when the facts show a decision or implementation change.

### Questions and asks

- Which user evidence drove this decision?
- How do we know the experience is clearer or more effective?
- What ambiguity or rework did design remove?
- Which quality tradeoff needs a decision?
- Shape the Ask around research, quality tradeoffs, design direction, staffing, or Product and Engineering alignment.

### Red flags

- Screens, iterations, or workshops reported as impact.
- Aesthetic preference presented as user evidence.
- Quality claims without criteria or validation.
- Design approval achieved without implementation ownership.

## VP Product: direction, prioritization, and roadmap consequence

### Core judgment

Decide whether the work improves product direction and portfolio outcomes by validating the right problem, sharpening priorities, and changing roadmap or investment decisions with credible customer and market evidence.

### Evidence hierarchy

1. Product outcome: user behavior, customer value, adoption, retention, conversion, or strategic learning.
2. Customer and market evidence supporting the problem and target segment.
3. Product direction: how the evidence confirms, changes, or challenges the strategy.
4. Prioritization tradeoff: what moves up, moves down, or is deferred and why.
5. Roadmap consequence, dependencies, and speed to validation.
6. Cross-functional feasibility and go-to-market implications that materially affect sequencing.

### Diagnostic

- **Accelerate:** evidence supports the problem, direction, and next investment.
- **Validate:** the opportunity is plausible, but customer, behavior, or market evidence is insufficient.
- **Resequence:** value exists, but dependencies, timing, or a higher-priority opportunity changes the roadmap order.
- **Deprioritize:** evidence does not support the problem, segment, differentiation, or expected product outcome.

### Frame the report

- Lead with the product judgment and what changed in direction or priority.
- Connect feature-level work to a product outcome or learning; omit detail that does neither.
- Make prioritization explicit by naming the displaced or deferred work when supplied.
- Distinguish customer signal, market signal, and observed product behavior.

### Questions and asks

- Why this problem, for this segment, now?
- What evidence should change the roadmap?
- What did we learn that affects product direction?
- What must be deprioritized or resequenced?
- Shape the Ask around product direction, roadmap priority, target segment, sequencing, or investment tradeoff.

### Red flags

- Feature delivery presented as product strategy.
- Priority justified only by internal enthusiasm or a single anecdote.
- New roadmap work added without an explicit tradeoff.
- Validation planned after a large irreversible investment.

## Cross-role tension checks

When a decision has a real tradeoff, keep the primary lens and expose the tension in `Challenges / Risks` or `Manager Questions`:

- **CPO vs. CMO:** useful product versus market awareness and demand.
- **CEO vs. CFO:** strategic option value versus measured financial return.
- **COO vs. CTO:** delivery timing versus technical sustainability.
- **CEO vs. CPO:** speed to scale versus confidence in product evidence.
- **CPO vs. VP Product:** product principles and user value versus portfolio sequencing and roadmap consequence.
- **VP Design vs. VP Product:** experience quality and user evidence versus product timing and portfolio priority.

Do not resolve these tensions by inventing consensus. State the known evidence, the unresolved tradeoff, and the decision owner.
